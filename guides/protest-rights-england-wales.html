Protest rights and police powers in England and Wales

Peaceful protest is protected by freedom of expression and freedom of assembly, but police can impose lawful restrictions and Parliament has created specific protest-related offences.

Core pointA protest is not unlawful just because it is disruptive or unpopular. But conduct can cross into an offence, and police may impose conditions when statutory thresholds are met.

Police conditions on protests

Sections 12, 14 and 14ZA of the Public Order Act 1986 allow conditions on processions, assemblies and one-person protests in specified circumstances, including risks of serious public disorder, serious property damage, serious disruption, intimidation and certain noise impacts. The Crime and Policing Act 2026 also requires account to be taken of cumulative disruption from repeated protests and adds powers relating to intimidating protests near places of worship.

Serious disruption and protest offences

The Public Order Act 2023 created offences including locking-on, going equipped to lock-on, tunnelling-related offences, obstruction of major transport works and interference with key national infrastructure. Some offences include a reasonable-excuse defence; whether it applies depends on the facts.

Stop and search at protests

The Public Order Act 2023 allows suspicion-led searches for items connected with protest-related offences and, when properly authorised, suspicionless stop and search in a specified locality. PACE Code A sets safeguards around these powers.

Roads, public nuisance and obstruction

Blocking roads or intentionally interfering with lawful activity can engage offences such as wilful obstruction of the highway or public nuisance, depending on the conduct and level of disruption.

Masks, balaclavas and identity concealment

England and Wales now has a specific 2026 offence for using an item to conceal identity inside a police-designated protest locality. See the dedicated face-coverings guide for the exact conditions and exceptions.

Flares, fireworks and pyrotechnics

The Crime and Policing Act 2026 created a protest-specific offence relating to possession of pyrotechnic articles by protest participants. Separate explosives, fireworks and public-order laws may also apply depending on the item and conduct.

Other 2026 protest changes

The 2026 Act also created or strengthened rules around climbing specified memorials, protests outside the homes of public-office holders, intimidating protests near places of worship, cumulative disruption and which senior officers can impose conditions.
