Protest rights, marches and demonstrations in Scotland

Police Scotland recognises the right to peaceful protest. The legal framework differs from England and Wales, especially for processions and the newer 2026 protest offences.

Core pointOrganised public processions in Scotland normally require advance notification. Static demonstrations do not have the same statutory advance-notice requirement under the Civic Government (Scotland) Act 1982.

Marches and public processions

Under the Civic Government (Scotland) Act 1982 as amended, organisers normally must notify the relevant local authority and Police Scotland at least 28 days before a march or parade. The notice covers matters including date, time, route, expected numbers and arrangements for control or stewarding.

Can a council impose conditions or prohibit a procession?

Yes. Local authorities can impose conditions or prohibit a public procession after considering statutory factors such as public safety, public order, property damage and disruption to the life of the community, following consultation with police.

Police conditions

Police Scotland can also use powers under section 12 of the Public Order Act 1986 to place conditions on a procession where the statutory threshold is met, including serious public disorder, serious damage to property, serious disruption or intimidation.

Static demonstrations

Scottish Government guidance says there is no advance-notification obligation for a static demonstration under the Civic Government (Scotland) Act 1982. Police can nevertheless impose conditions under section 14 of the Public Order Act 1986 where the statutory test is met.

Masks and balaclavas

The specific Crime and Policing Act 2026 protest identity-concealment offence introduced for England and Wales does not generally extend to Scotland. That does not mean any use of a mask is always lawful: other criminal laws, police powers, event conditions or evidence of intent can still be relevant.

Violence, disorder and criminal offences

Police Scotland says it will act where offences are committed. Protest does not provide immunity from offences involving violence, property damage, threatening conduct, hate crime or other criminal behaviour.
