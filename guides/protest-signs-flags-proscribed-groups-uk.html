Flags, signs, chants and proscribed organisations at protests

Freedom of expression protects a wide range of political speech, including speech that is controversial or unpopular. But some expression can cross into criminal offences, particularly where a proscribed terrorist organisation is involved.

Core pointA flag, logo, item of clothing, sign or chant is not judged in isolation. The words, image, surrounding circumstances, intent and the organisation involved can all matter.

Inviting support for a proscribed organisation

Section 12 of the Terrorism Act 2000 makes it an offence to invite support for a proscribed organisation. GOV.UK explains that the support can include moral support or approval, not only money or property.

Expressing supportive opinions

The Terrorism Act also covers expressing an opinion or belief supportive of a proscribed organisation where the person is reckless as to whether someone hearing or receiving it will be encouraged to support the organisation.

Flags, clothing, logos and other articles

Section 13 makes it an offence in public to wear clothing or carry or display an article in such a way or in circumstances that arouse reasonable suspicion that the person is a member or supporter of a proscribed organisation. The law can also apply to publishing an image of such an item in the specified circumstances.

How do I know whether an organisation is proscribed?

The Home Office maintains the official list of proscribed terrorist organisations. Names and aliases matter, and the list can change, so check the current official version rather than relying on social media.

What about offensive or hateful chants?

Separate public-order and hate-crime laws may apply to threatening, abusive or hatred-stirring words or material. The exact offences differ between England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. For example, Scotland uses the Hate Crime and Public Order (Scotland) Act 2021 for its current stirring-up framework.

Does criticism of a government or support for a political cause automatically amount to terrorism support?

No. Criticism of governments, advocacy for political change and discussion of conflicts are not automatically offences. The Terrorism Act provisions concern the statutory tests around proscribed organisations and the particular conduct or expression.
